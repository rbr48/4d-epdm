# Japan's Rise as an Economic Superpower, and What It Does and Does Not Tell Us About Bangladesh

## What this document is, and what it deliberately is not

This is a comparative-development analysis: a historical account of the mechanisms behind
Japan's rise, a diagnostic of Bangladesh's actual current binding constraints using real
data, an honest assessment of which Japanese mechanisms could plausibly transfer, and a
sequenced policy roadmap.

**It is not a forecast.** Nothing in this document assigns a probability or a date to
Bangladesh reaching any income level or "superpower" status. That claim was tried earlier
in this project, in the form of a 9-dimensional capability index with Monte Carlo
trajectories to 2044 — and it failed on every honest test applied to it, including one
where the model was handed the *correct future state* and still lost to a random walk.
Comparative history and growth diagnostics are a different, older, and better-established
tool for this question than point forecasting ever was, and this document uses that tool
instead.

**On sourcing.** Two disciplines are applied throughout, both earned the hard way earlier
in this project:

1. Where a claim is genuinely contested among economic historians, it is presented as
   contested, with the competing view named — not resolved by picking whichever version
   sounds better.
2. No number is stated to a precision this document can defend. An earlier version of
   this project cited a specific model-internal decomposition ("technology-adoption
   barriers explain ~40% of the postwar miracle") as if it were a measured historical
   fact, when it was a figure from inside one paper's own calibrated model. That error is
   not repeated here: where a number is a broad, textbook-level consensus (e.g., Japan's
   high-growth-era real growth rate), it is stated as such; where it would require a
   citation this document cannot verify in this pass, it is flagged rather than asserted.

**On currency.** The quantitative panel used throughout (World Bank WDI, Worldwide
Governance Indicators via source=3, Penn World Table 10.01) runs through 2024 and was
downloaded fresh for this analysis — every number attributed to "the data" below can be
traced to it. Anything about Bangladesh's political situation after that point rests on
this author's general knowledge rather than the verified panel and is flagged as such
where it matters.

# Part I — Japan's Rise: Mechanisms, Not a Story

## 1.1 The Meiji foundation (1868–1912): the part usually skipped

The postwar "miracle" narrative often starts in 1955, which erases the seventy years of
institution-building that made 1955 possible. The Meiji government, after 1868, moved
fast and specifically:

- **Abolished the feudal domain system** and centralized fiscal and administrative
  authority in a modern state — removing the fragmented, locally-captured governance
  structure that precedes state-directed development almost everywhere it fails.
- **Built compulsory universal education** starting in the 1870s, reaching near-universal
  primary enrollment well before most of the developing world in the twentieth century.
  This is the single most load-bearing and least glamorous fact in the entire Japan case:
  by the time rapid industrial catch-up became possible, Japan already had a literate,
  numerate workforce able to absorb it.
- **Imported foreign technical advisors and sent students abroad** deliberately and at
  state expense, then systematically replaced foreign experts with trained nationals —
  an early, explicit technology-absorption strategy rather than technology invention.
- **Built state-owned pilot industries**, then privatized many of them to politically
  connected merchant houses (the origin of the zaibatsu), establishing a pattern —
  state-seeded, privately-operated heavy industry — that recurs through the twentieth
  century.

The mechanism worth naming: **Japan entered the twentieth century already more capable
than most economies at a comparable income level**, because state capacity and mass
education were built as a first move, not a byproduct of growth. This matters directly
for Part III: a country that has not done this yet cannot skip to the 1955 playbook.

## 1.2 Destruction and reset (1937–1952)

Japan's wartime economy over-concentrated in armaments, and Allied bombing and the loss
of colonial resource access left the economy severely damaged by 1945; postwar
reconstructions place the immediate postwar output well below prewar levels, with recovery
to prewar output per capita achieved by the early 1950s. The exact percentage figures
vary by source and are not restated here to a precision this document cannot verify.

What matters more than the destruction is what the postwar occupation did with the
reset, because it is the part of the story with the clearest causal claim and the
strongest scholarly consensus:

- **Land reform (1946–1950)** transferred land from a landlord class to owner-cultivators
  at scale, breaking a rural elite that had captured a large share of agricultural
  surplus and had limited investment incentives. This is broadly credited with both
  raising agricultural productivity and removing a politically entrenched veto group over
  rural policy — a genuine, dated, comparatively unusual episode of a government
  redistributing an incumbent elite's asset base by policy rather than by revolution.
- **Zaibatsu dissolution**, intended to break the prewar industrial conglomerates'
  concentrated ownership, was only partially completed before Cold War priorities shifted
  U.S. occupation policy toward reindustrialization; the conglomerates reconstituted as
  bank-centered keiretsu with cross-shareholding rather than being permanently broken up.
- **Labor law reform** legalized independent unions and established the enterprise-union,
  lifetime-employment model that later became central to Japanese industrial relations —
  a specific institutional choice, not an inevitable one, that shaped how productivity
  gains were shared and how firms retained skilled workers through cycles.
- **A new constitution and Article 9** capped Japan's military spending far below what a
  state of its size would otherwise carry, freeing fiscal capacity for civilian investment
  — a structural fact with real quantitative weight that is often underweighted in
  "miracle" narratives because it is external to the model, not a policy choice Japan
  made unilaterally.

## 1.3 The high-growth era (1955–1973): what actually happened, and what is contested

The uncontested part: real GNP growth over roughly 1955–1973 was extraordinary by any
historical standard — sustained annual rates in the high single digits to low double
digits for the better part of two decades, a pace few economies have matched over a
comparable span. This is close to textbook consensus and is restated here only at that
level of precision.

The mechanisms, in descending order of how well-established their causal contribution is:

**High and rising domestic savings, channeled through a bank-centered financial
system.** Japan's household savings rate and gross domestic investment rate were
exceptionally high through this period (investment above 30% of GDP for an extended
stretch — a level Bangladesh, notably, already exceeds today at 30.7%, which is discussed
in Part III). Credit allocation ran substantially through banks with close relationships
to the Ministry of Finance and the Bank of Japan, giving the state real leverage over
where capital went without formal nationalization.

**Technology import and adaptation, not frontier invention.** Japanese firms licensed
foreign technology extensively through the 1950s and 1960s, then invested heavily in
process improvement — quality control methods associated with W. Edwards Deming became
embedded in Japanese manufacturing practice during this period and are widely credited
with the shift from "cheap" to "reliable" as the reputation of Japanese exports. The
sequence — import, adapt, improve, eventually innovate — is a genuinely replicable
pattern in principle, distinct from requiring frontier R&D capacity from the outset.

**Export discipline as an information and accountability mechanism.** Firms and sectors
that received policy support were expected, in the strongest accounts of this period, to
demonstrate competitiveness in export markets — a mechanism that forces continuous
productivity improvement because international buyers, unlike domestic protected
markets, do not have to buy an inferior product.

**A fixed, and by the late 1960s undervalued, exchange rate under Bretton Woods** (360
yen to the dollar until 1971) gave Japanese exporters a durable price advantage that
does not depend on any domestic policy choice and cannot be replicated by policy choice
today, because the fixed-rate international system that made it possible no longer
exists.

**U.S. Cold War strategic interest** gave Japan preferential access to the American
market, technology transfer, and a security guarantee that suppressed defense spending —
a geopolitical tailwind specific to Japan's position in postwar Asia, not a mechanism
available to a state seeking development today under different alliance structures.

**Rural-to-urban labor reallocation**, moving workers from lower-productivity
agriculture into higher-productivity manufacturing, contributed measurably to
aggregate productivity growth during this period, a pattern common to most successful
industrializations and directly relevant to Bangladesh's current structural position
(discussed in Part III).

**The contested part — industrial policy's actual causal weight.** The popular account,
most associated with Chalmers Johnson's *MITI and the Japanese Miracle* (1982), holds
that Japan's Ministry of International Trade and Industry (MITI) deliberately identified
and promoted strategic sectors through credit allocation, import protection, and
coordination, and that this targeting was a primary driver of the miracle — the
"developmental state" thesis. This account is influential and shaped how many other
countries have since tried to imitate Japan.

It is also disputed. Economists including Richard Beason and David Weinstein, examining
which sectors actually received the heaviest MITI support and how those sectors performed
relative to others, found limited evidence that the most heavily targeted sectors grew
faster or became more competitive than less-targeted ones — suggesting industrial policy
may have followed and coordinated market-driven success more than it caused it, or that
its net effect on growth was smaller than the popular narrative implies. This document
does not resolve that dispute. What can be said without resolving it: the *institutional
architecture* MITI represented — a technically capable civil service, sustained
coordination between state and firms, and close monitoring of firm performance — is a
more defensible transferable lesson than the specific claim that *picking winners*
worked, because the architecture claim survives even if the winner-picking claim does
not.

## 1.4 Maturation and the Lost Decades (1973–present): the warning built into the case

Growth slowed sharply after the 1973 oil shock as catch-up opportunities diminished, and
the fixed exchange rate advantage ended with the 1971 collapse of Bretton Woods. The 1985
Plaza Accord forced yen appreciation, and the late-1980s asset price bubble in equities
and real estate — followed by its 1990–91 collapse — produced a multi-decade period of
low growth, deflation, and balance-sheet repair now commonly called the Lost Decades.
Japan's population has been aging and, since 2008, declining, compounding the growth
slowdown structurally rather than cyclically.

**Two disciplines follow from this, both directly relevant to a 25-year Bangladesh plan
and both largely absent from earlier drafts of this project's work:**

1. A development model has to contain a mechanism for the transition *from* catch-up
   growth *to* frontier-adjacent growth, because catch-up growth mechanically runs out as
   the gap to the frontier closes. Japan did not solve this transition well; that is a
   genuine part of the case, not an inconvenient footnote to leave out.
2. Demographic transitions are not one-directional gifts. The same dependency-ratio
   dynamic that helped Japan's miracle (a large working-age cohort relative to
   dependents) inverted into the aging dependency burden now weighing on Japan's current
   growth. Bangladesh's dependency ratio is *currently* falling — the dividend is live —
   but the data (§2.4 below) show the window is finite, and Japan's own trajectory is the
   clearest illustration in this dataset of what happens after it closes without a
   completed transition to frontier-adjacent productivity.

# Part II — Bangladesh: A Growth-Diagnostics Read of the Actual Current Situation

## 2.1 Why growth diagnostics, and not another capability index

The Hausmann–Rodrik–Velasco growth diagnostics framework (2005) asks a narrower and more
answerable question than "what capability index would predict this country's future": it
asks *what is currently the single most binding constraint* on private investment and
growth, by working down a decision tree — is investment low because the *return* to
investment is low (poor complementary factors: infrastructure, human capital,
geography), or because the return exists but is not *appropriable* (macro instability,
government failures, market failures, corruption), or because finance is *expensive or
unavailable* (weak domestic financial intermediation, poor access to international
capital)? This is the right tool for a diagnostic chapter because it does not require
estimating a global functional form across a panel too small to identify one — it asks
where a specific economy's constraint sits, using the standard indicators for each branch
directly.

## 2.2 Working the decision tree for Bangladesh

**Is the problem a low return to investment?** The data argue no, or at least not as the
primary constraint. Investment is already at 30.7% of GDP (2024) — higher than the
U.S. (21.5%), Germany (21.5%), the U.K. (19.2%), and Japan's own current rate (27.7%),
and comparable to Korea (30.0%) and China (40.5%). Capital is being mobilized at a rate
that is not, on its face, the binding constraint. This is consistent with Bangladesh's
well-documented investment-led growth of the last two decades and argues against
"Bangladesh needs more capital formation" as the headline diagnosis — it already has a
high capital-formation rate by international standards; the question is what that capital
is producing.

**Is the problem appropriability — government or market failures eroding the private
return?** This is where the data point hardest. Bangladesh's four Worldwide Governance
Indicators are all negative and, as of the 2024 data, among the weakest in this
16-country panel:

| Indicator | Bangladesh 2024 | Panel range (16 countries) |
|---|---|---|
| Government effectiveness | −0.62 | +2.28 (Singapore) to −0.62 (Bangladesh, lowest) |
| Regulatory quality | −0.95 | +2.13 (Singapore) to −0.95 (Bangladesh, lowest) |
| Rule of law | −0.96 | +1.71 (Netherlands) to −0.96 (Bangladesh, lowest) |
| Control of corruption | −1.10 | +1.97 (Singapore) to −1.10 (Bangladesh, lowest) |

Bangladesh scores **lowest of all sixteen countries in this panel on every one of the
four governance dimensions**, including below Philippines, Indonesia, and Vietnam, all of
which are common comparators at similar or lower income levels. This is not a marginal
finding; it is the single sharpest signal in the entire dataset. A high investment rate
combined with the weakest governance scores in the comparison set is consistent with a
diagnosis in which capital is being deployed, but a meaningful share of its return is
being captured, diverted, or destroyed by governance failure rather than reaching
productive, reinvestable use.

**Is the problem cost of finance?** Partially, and in a specific way: credit to the
private sector as a share of GDP is 35.8% — low relative to Korea (160%), the U.S.
(196%), or even Thailand (146%) and Philippines (50%), indicating a comparatively shallow
domestic financial system. This is compounded by what the earlier phase of this project
established from other sources (not in this panel): Bangladesh's banking sector has
carried a materially elevated non-performing loan ratio in recent years, which — if
accurate at the scale reported elsewhere — would mean the shallow credit market that
exists is also impaired, doubly constraining the financial-intermediation channel. This
document flags that NPL figure as sourced outside the verified panel and in need of a
current citation before being used in any published version of this analysis, consistent
with the sourcing discipline stated at the top.

**Reading the constraints together.** The evidence points most strongly toward
**appropriability** — governance, corruption, and rule-of-law weakness — as the primary
binding constraint, with a secondary, likely related constraint in **financial-sector
depth and quality**. It does not point toward capital scarcity, and it does not point
toward a human-capital-first story either (see §2.3). This has a direct, non-obvious
policy implication: pouring more capital into infrastructure or industrial capacity
without addressing the governance and financial-sector constraints is likely to
reproduce the exact O-Ring-style pattern this project's earlier work gestured at but
never actually demonstrated with data — investment whose return is capped by the
weakest link, which the data say is institutional, not physical.

## 2.3 The trade-openness reversal: a fact worth explaining, not smoothing over

Bangladesh's trade openness (exports plus imports as a share of GDP) fell from 42.1% in
2015 to 26.8% in 2024 — a large move in the wrong direction for an economy whose growth
model has been built on export manufacturing. Several non-exclusive explanations are
consistent with known events and should be distinguished rather than collapsed into one
story:

- The 2022–24 balance-of-payments and reserves stress (reserves fell to roughly 3 months
  of import cover by 2024 in this data, itself a low external buffer) is associated with
  import compression — a country facing a reserves crunch imports less, which mechanically
  lowers the trade-openness ratio even if exports are stable.
- Global ready-made-garment demand and pricing conditions in this period were not
  uniformly favorable across competing exporters.
- A currency and exchange-rate management story: how the taka was valued and managed
  over this period affects the dollar-denominated trade ratio independent of real
  quantities traded.

This document does not adjudicate between these explanations with the data at hand — that
would require a decomposition this panel is not built to support — but flags the
reversal as a fact that any credible Bangladesh strategy has to explain and address
directly, because "export-led growth" as a strategy is in some tension with a trade ratio
that has fallen by more than a third in under a decade.

## 2.4 The demographic dividend: real, currently open, and dated

Bangladesh's dependency ratio fell from 75.4 (1996) to 52.6 (2024) — a genuine, large,
already-realized shift toward a higher share of working-age population, consistent with
the fertility decline that is one of Bangladesh's most credited development successes.
This is a real asset, not a projection.

It is also a **closing** window, on the same demographic-transition logic that
eventually inverted for Japan (§1.4) and for Korea, both now well into population aging.
Exactly when Bangladesh's dependency ratio troughs and begins rising is a projection this
document does not attempt to pin to a specific year with false precision, but the
direction is not in question: every economy in this panel that industrialized earlier has
already passed through this same trough and out the other side into rising dependency.
The policy implication is about **urgency of sequencing**, not about a doomsday date: the
dividend is an asset with a shelf life, and the highest-value use of a falling dependency
ratio is building the productive capacity, skills base, and pension/social-insurance
infrastructure *while* the ratio is still favorable, not after it turns.

## 2.5 What Bangladesh has actually already achieved, stated plainly

It is easy, in a document built around identifying constraints, to understate what has
already happened. It should not be understated: GDP per capita (PPP) rose more than
threefold between 1996 and 2024 in this data; investment rates rose from the low
twenties to over 30% of GDP; the dependency ratio fell by nearly 23 points; and this
occurred alongside well-documented, if not directly present in this economic panel,
gains in poverty reduction, primary education access, child mortality, and female labor
force participation. Bangladesh's transformation since 1996 — and since independence in
1971 more broadly — is a genuine development success story by the standard measures. The
diagnosis in this section is about what is now the binding constraint on the *next*
phase, not a claim that nothing has worked.

# Part III — What Transfers From Japan, What Doesn't, and Why That Distinction Matters

## 3.1 Mechanisms that plausibly transfer

**Technology absorption over frontier invention.** Japan's sequence — import, license,
adapt, improve — did not require frontier R&D capacity as a precondition, and neither
does a Bangladesh strategy. The lesson transfers as a *sequencing principle*: prioritize
absorptive capacity (technical education, firm-level quality management, standards
certification) before frontier research investment, which has a much longer and less
certain payoff at Bangladesh's current position.

**Rural-to-urban productivity reallocation.** This mechanism is already partially active
in Bangladesh's own transformation (agriculture's share of the economy has fallen as
manufacturing and services have grown) and is a continuing, not a new, lever — the
diagnostic question is whether reallocation is still proceeding efficiently or is now
constrained by urban infrastructure, housing, and labor-market frictions.

**Export discipline as an accountability mechanism**, in principle, transfers regardless
of era — a firm that must compete in international markets faces a harder performance
constraint than one protected in a domestic market. What does *not* automatically
transfer is the specific channel (a fixed, undervalued exchange rate) that made this
especially powerful for Japan; Bangladesh would need to achieve export discipline through
competitiveness and market access rather than currency policy, because a comparable fixed
exchange-rate regime is not available in the current international system.

**Institutional architecture over specific institutional form.** The Meiji-era lesson —
build state capacity and mass education before or alongside rapid growth, not after — is
directly relevant and, per §2.2, points at exactly the constraint the data identify as
binding for Bangladesh today. This is the single most transferable lesson in the entire
Japan case, and it is a lesson about *sequencing*, not about copying Japan's specific
ministries or industrial-policy instruments.

## 3.2 Mechanisms that do not transfer, and should not be assumed

**The Cold War geopolitical tailwind.** Preferential U.S. market access, technology
transfer, and a security guarantee that suppressed Japan's defense spending were specific
to Japan's position in postwar Asia. Bangladesh's current geopolitical position — no
equivalent patron relationship, and a graduation from Least Developed Country status that
*removes* rather than grants trade preferences (§4.2) — is close to the opposite
structural position.

**The fixed exchange-rate export subsidy.** Bretton Woods no longer exists. Any export
competitiveness Bangladesh builds has to come from productivity and market access, not
currency undervaluation locked in by an international monetary system that ended in 1971.

**A pre-existing high-capacity state and near-universal literacy at the point growth
accelerates.** Japan's rapid catch-up phase began from an already Meiji-modernized
institutional and educational base built over the preceding seventy years. Bangladesh is
attempting institutional strengthening *concurrently* with growth, not as a completed
precondition — a harder sequencing problem, and one that should be named as harder rather
than assumed away.

**A less competitive global manufacturing landscape.** Japan's 1955 export push faced a
world with far fewer credible manufacturing-export competitors than exist today.
Bangladesh (and every other current aspirant) competes in a far more crowded field —
Vietnam, in this very panel, shows a trade-openness ratio of 173.9% against Bangladesh's
26.8%, illustrating that the "become an export powerhouse" playbook is being run by
direct competitors right now, not merely as a historical reference point.

**Climate vulnerability as a novel structural constraint Japan never faced at comparable
severity.** Bangladesh's exposure to sea-level rise, cyclones, and riverine flooding is a
structural drag on infrastructure durability and a recurring capital-destruction shock
that has no equivalent in Japan's development-era case. Any Bangladesh-specific plan has
to treat climate adaptation as a first-order investment category, not an add-on.

## 3.3 The most directly useful comparator may not be Japan

Within this project's own data, **Vietnam** — not Japan — is the more structurally
similar and more currently relevant comparator: a broadly comparable starting income
level in recent decades, a similarly high investment rate (30.6% vs. Bangladesh's
30.7%), but a trade-openness ratio more than six times higher and governance indicators
that, while still negative, sit closer to zero than Bangladesh's across all four
dimensions. Vietnam is running a version of the export-led, technology-absorption
playbook *right now*, under current global trade conditions, from a broadly similar
starting point. **South Korea** offers a second, later, and in some respects more
transferable version of the "developmental state" architecture than Japan's own 1950s
case, having achieved its catch-up under conditions closer to the present international
system than Japan's Bretton Woods-era experience. A rigorous Bangladesh strategy should
treat Japan as the deepest historical case for institutional-sequencing lessons, and
Vietnam and Korea as the more directly comparable operational templates for what is
achievable under current conditions.

# Part IV — A Sequenced Strategy, Not a Forecast

Everything in this section is a policy-reasoning claim, not a quantitative prediction.
No probability, date, or index value is attached to any outcome. The sequencing logic
follows directly from Part II's diagnosis (appropriability/governance and financial-depth
are the binding constraints, not capital scarcity) and Part III's transfer analysis
(technology absorption and institutional architecture transfer; currency-based export
subsidy and geopolitical tailwinds do not).

## Phase 1 (near-term): address the binding constraint directly

The growth-diagnostics read in §2.2 says plainly that pouring more capital into physical
investment is not the first-order lever — Bangladesh already invests at a rate above
Japan's current level. The first-order levers are the ones the data flag as weakest:

- **Financial-sector repair.** Address non-performing loans and strengthen banking
  supervision before expanding credit access further; a shallow *and* impaired financial
  system compounds rather than solves the intermediation constraint identified in §2.2.
- **Governance and anti-corruption measures targeted at investment-relevant
  institutions** — contract enforcement, regulatory predictability, customs and port
  administration — rather than broad, diffuse "institutional reform" that is harder to
  sequence or measure. The data do not specify *which* governance failure matters most
  for investment; that requires firm-level survey evidence (e.g., World Bank Enterprise
  Surveys) this panel does not contain, and is flagged as a needed next input rather than
  assumed.
- **LDC-graduation transition management.** Bangladesh's graduation from Least Developed
  Country status removes preferential trade access this document cannot independently
  verify the exact current date or terms of from the panel alone (see §4.2's flag on
  currency), but the direction — loss of preference, not gain — is a known, dated
  structural shock requiring an explicit transition plan (tariff-equivalent competitiveness
  measures, trade-agreement negotiation, or both) rather than treatment as a distant
  contingency.

## Phase 2 (medium-term): build absorptive capacity, not frontier capacity

Following the Japan-derived sequencing principle (§3.1): prioritize technical and
vocational education, quality-management capability at the firm level, and standards
certification over investment in frontier research. This is where the closing
demographic window (§2.4) is most directly relevant — building an engineering and
technical-skills base is a multi-year undertaking that has to start while the working-age
share of the population is still rising, not after.

## Phase 3 (medium-to-long-term): diversify beyond the current export base

The trade-openness reversal (§2.3) and the RMG-export concentration this project's
earlier phases established argue for deliberate diversification — but "deliberate"
should mean discovery-oriented (support, measure, scale or exit — the same 4A logic
Part III's institutional-architecture lesson implies) rather than picking specific
sectors by assertion, given the genuine scholarly uncertainty (§1.3) about how well
sector-picking actually worked even in Japan's own case.

## Phase 4 (long-term): the transition Japan itself did not manage well

Part I's discussion of the Lost Decades is not included as a coda — it is a direct input
to sequencing. A plan that only addresses catch-up growth and has no mechanism for the
transition to frontier-adjacent productivity is repeating the gap in Japan's own
trajectory, not avoiding it. This phase is necessarily the least specific in this
document, because it is furthest from current, verifiable data — which is stated here
as a limitation, not resolved by asserting a confident-sounding plan for it.

In the interest of not repeating this project's own earlier pattern of presenting
unfinished work as complete:

1. **The current (2025–2026) Bangladesh policy and political situation** is not verified
   against a live source in this pass. Anything in this document touching current events
   beyond what the 2024-vintage panel data show should be treated as this author's general
   background knowledge, not as sourced fact, until checked.
2. **The specific NPL ratio and tax-to-GDP figures** referenced qualitatively in §2.2 came
   from outside the verified panel in an earlier phase of this project and need a current,
   cited source before appearing in any published version.
3. **Firm-level evidence on which specific governance failure binds hardest** (contract
   enforcement vs. customs administration vs. regulatory predictability, etc.) is not in
   this panel and would meaningfully sharpen §2.2 and Phase 1 if added — World Bank
   Enterprise Surveys are the standard source for this.
4. **The LDC graduation date and terms** should be confirmed against a current UN CDP or
   WTO source rather than restated from memory.
5. This document has not yet been reconciled with `RESEARCH_PAPER_AND_DISSERTATION_CHAPTER.md`
   in this repository, which contains real, independently-verified out-of-sample
   forecasting results (Engine 1 / NCD-LP) that are a genuine, separate, and legitimate
   piece of this project's work — the short-horizon econometric forecaster passes the
   pre-registered test this document's own governing project established, and nothing in
   this document should be read as walking that back. Sections 6–9 of that paper (the 9D
   capability index and 2044 Monte Carlo trajectories) should be superseded by this
   document's Parts I–IV rather than read alongside them as an equally-valid alternative.
